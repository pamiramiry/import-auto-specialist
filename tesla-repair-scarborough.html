Tesla Diagnostics With Toolbox 3

We run Toolbox 3, Tesla's own diagnostic software. That is the difference between working on a Tesla and working around one.

A Tesla is far less accessible to generic equipment than a combustion car. There is no OBD-II port doing anything useful in the traditional sense, and the systems that matter — the battery management system, the drive units, the thermal management loop, the charging hardware, the body controllers — report through Tesla's own interface. Toolbox 3 reads those systems directly, runs Tesla's diagnostic routines, and performs the procedures a repair requires afterwards.

Without it, an independent shop is limited to what can be done mechanically and is guessing at everything else. With it, you get a real diagnosis rather than a referral.

High-Voltage Battery and Drive Unit Work

We take on high-voltage work — battery and drive unit diagnosis and repair — which is the line most independent shops will not cross.

That covers diagnosing battery and charging faults, drive unit noise and failure, thermal management and coolant loop problems, and charging issues at the port or the onboard charger. High-voltage systems are not something to approach casually: the car is de-energised and the system isolated before any HV work begins, and the diagnosis is done with Toolbox 3 rather than inferred.

It is worth knowing what this means practically. A fault a service centre would resolve by replacing a complete assembly can sometimes be diagnosed down to a specific component, and out of warranty that difference is substantial. Why Tesla Brakes Need Attention Here

This is the most misunderstood thing about running an EV in this climate, and it catches a lot of Tesla owners out.

Regenerative braking does most of your slowing, so the friction brakes are barely used. Owners hear "the brakes last forever" and stop thinking about them. In a salt-belt winter the opposite problem develops: pads and rotors that are rarely applied sit and corrode. Calipers seize, slide pins bind, and rotors build a rust layer that never gets scrubbed off, because the friction brakes are hardly working.

  • A grinding or scraping noise that appears after the car has sat
  • The car pulling to one side under hard braking
  • A brake warning, or a reduced regenerative braking message
  • Visible heavy rust across the rotor faces, not just the edges
  • A seized caliper, or one wheel noticeably hotter than the others

Brake service on a Tesla in Scarborough is less about wear and more about corrosion and seizure. Cleaning and lubricating the caliper slides periodically is genuinely preventative here, and it costs very little next to replacing seized calipers.

Tires, Alignment and the Weight Problem

Teslas are heavy and deliver torque instantly, and tires pay for both. Owners routinely get far less life from a set than they expect, and uneven wear shows up faster than on a comparable combustion car.

Alignment matters more as a result, not less. A small alignment error that would cost a lighter car some tread over years will strip a Tesla's tires noticeably in a single season. We mount and balance, read wear patterns for what they say about the suspension, and align to specification. Pothole damage is worth taking seriously on these cars too — the combination of weight and low-profile tires puts a lot of any impact straight into the wheel and suspension.

The 12-Volt Battery Nobody Expects

Almost every Tesla owner is surprised the car still has a conventional low-voltage battery, and it is one of the most common reasons a Tesla stops working entirely.

When it fails the car can refuse to wake, the doors and screen may not respond, and you can find yourself locked out of a vehicle whose main battery is nearly full. It usually gives warning first — a "12V battery needs service" alert, or odd electrical behaviour — and it is a straightforward job caught early and a tow when it is not. If your car has shown that alert, do not put it off.

Body, Collision and Everything Else

Tesla body repair is a well-known pain point: long waits and limited options. Mechanical and collision work are both done here, and the car can be scanned with Toolbox 3 as part of the same job — which matters, because cameras, sensors and radar hardware sit directly behind the panels that get damaged.

Beyond that, Teslas need the ordinary things every car needs, and those are easy to forget when there is no oil change bringing you in twice a year: suspension components, cabin air filters, A/C and heat pump service, wipers and glass, and coolant for the thermal management system. No oil changes does not mean no maintenance.
